Deep relations · IRS SOI × Census × SAIPE

Florida gained $20.6 billion in income last year without anyone earning it there

Data: IRS filing years 2022–23 · SAIPE 2023 · pulled 2026-08-24

The IRS quietly publishes where every tax return moved between filings, with adjusted gross income attached. The 2022–23 vintage says the great income migration is real and lopsided: Florida netted +$20.6B in AGI from interstate movers — nearly 4× second-place Texas — while California lost $11.9B (about $304 per resident) and New York lost $9.9B.

  • The movers are rich: Florida's inbound returns averaged $122,539 AGI — roughly 1.67× Florida's own resident median household income ($73,283). New York's leavers averaged $105,983, 1.29× the New York median. The people in motion out-earn the people in place, on both ends.
  • Sun Belt sweep: the top five gainers are FL, TX, SC, NC, AZ; the top five losers are CA, NY, IL, MA, NJ.
  • Every figure reproduced exactly from fresh downloads of the IRS inflow/outflow files, including the end-anchored filter that separates "Total Migration-US" from its lookalike summary rows.

2022–23 filing-year vintage — flows lag today by several years. Mean AGI per return ≠ median household income (different unit, different concept, mean skews high); the 1.67× is directional. IRS suppresses tiny flows.

Deep relations · USGS × city geography × Census

America's most famous earthquake zones had zero earthquakes

Data: Aug 2001 – Aug 2026 · Census vintage 2024 · pulled 2026-08-24

Count every M4.5+ earthquake within 200 km of twelve major metros over 25 years and rank them. Anchorage: 159 — including the M7.1 that hit the city in November 2018. San Diego: 100. Los Angeles: 57. And the two most famous "Big One" zones outside California? Memphis and St. Louis, sitting on the New Madrid fault: zero. Each. Portland and Seattle, atop M9-capable Cascadia: four and six.

  • The inference: background frequency is not worst-case risk. Cascadia and New Madrid store their energy and spend it rarely, in enormous events — their quiet 25-year records are exactly what a locked fault looks like.
  • Los Angeles County puts 9.76 million people next to 2.3 quakes a year; Anchorage's 290k residents live with 6.4 a year.
  • The verifier went hunting for missed leaders and found one worth knowing: San Juan, Puerto Rico would rank #2 at 152 — and St. Louis's zero is boundary-sensitive (the 2008 M5.2 Mt. Carmel quake sits ~205 km out, just past the 200 km cutoff).

200 km circles overlap for LA/San Diego, so counts aren't additive. Core-county population understates metro size. Frequency ranking says nothing about building codes or shaking intensity.

Deep relations · CDC/NCHS × SAIPE × Census

Nine years of life separate Hawaii and Mississippi — and income tracks it at r = 0.79

Data: life expectancy 2021 · income 2023 · pulled 2026-08-24

Join every state's 2021 life expectancy at birth (NCHS) to its median household income (SAIPE) and the strongest correlation in this entire publication appears: r = 0.79. A newborn in Hawaii (79.9 years, $96,716 median income) is expected to outlive one in Mississippi (70.9 years, $54,386) by nine full years.

Hawaii 79.9 yrs · $96.7k Massachusetts 79.6 · $99.8k Connecticut 79.2 · $91.5k Alabama 72.0 · $62.2k W. Virginia 71.0 · $55.9k Mississippi 70.9 · $54.4k
  • The least-squares slope works out to roughly $6,800 of median household income per additional year of life expectancy — a correlation, emphatically not a price tag: income proxies education, healthcare access, smoking rates, and much else.
  • Interesting residual: DC has the highest income ($104,643) but only 75.3 years — and New York over-performs its income at 79.0.
  • Life expectancy is the COVID-depressed 2021 vintage (the most recent state-level NCHS release found); the r held at 0.786 under independent recomputation with a clean 51/51 state join.

2021 life expectancy vs 2023 income; unweighted across states; ecological correlation — says nothing about individuals.

Deep relations · CFPB × SAIPE × Census

We suspected poverty drives the complaint belt. The data says barely.

Data: complaints 2025 · income 2023 · population 2024 · pulled 2026-08-24

The complaint-per-capita map looks like a poverty map — Mississippi, Louisiana, Alabama all near the top. So we tested it: state median income vs 2025 complaints per 1,000 residents, all 51 jurisdictions. The correlation is r = −0.11 (−0.17 without outlier DC). Income explains 1–3% of the variance. The poorest ten states do average more complaints than the richest ten (14.2 vs 10.9 per 1,000), but that gap is carried almost entirely by one regional cluster.

  • The residuals name the real story: Georgia files 26 more complaints per 1,000 than its income predicts, Florida +22, Mississippi +21 — while equally poor West Virginia (3.5) and New Mexico (4.0) barely complain at all. Poverty doesn't produce complaint volume; a Southeast credit-dispute economy does.
  • Poorest-state cluster check: Mississippi 34.5/1,000 vs West Virginia 3.5/1,000 — a 10× gap between two states $1,500 apart in median income.
  • Every statistic — both correlations, both decile averages, the regression line, all 51 scatter values — reproduced exactly under independent recomputation.

Ecological correlation at state level; SAIPE 2023 income vs 2025 complaints vs 2024 population. The verifier also tightened one caveat: ~28k excluded complaints sit in territory/military buckets and another ~8k carry no state at all.

Deep relations · USASpending × Census × SAIPE

Federal award money per resident has no relationship to state income at all

Data: latest 12 months of award obligations · income 2023 · pulled 2026-08-24

USASpending totals the last twelve months of federal award obligations by recipient state; divide by population and correlate with income and you get the flattest line in this publication: r = −0.01. Rich and poor states alike sit at both extremes — the per-resident spread runs 14.6×, from North Dakota's $108,117 down to Utah's $7,404, with income explaining none of it.

  • The national picture: $4.57 trillion in awards over twelve months — $13,424 per resident.
  • North Dakota's wild number is the measure showing its seams: "recipient state" credits money to where the awarded entity sits, not where work happens — $86.1B landed on 797k residents, exceeding even DC's $58,406 per head.
  • Bottom five is its own argument against the income theory: New Jersey ($99.7k income) and Utah ($93k) sit beside Kansas, Nevada, and Georgia at under $9,000 per resident.

Award obligations exclude Social Security, Medicare benefits, and federal payroll — this is grant/contract money, not all federal spending. The API's exact 12-month window isn't stated in its response. All figures byte-identical on independent re-fetch.

Cross-inference · CFPB × Census

The complaint machine has a home address, and it's Georgia

Data: complaints 2019 and 2025 · population 2024 · pulled 2026-08-21

Divide 2025's 5.44M consumer complaints by each state's population and the geography snaps into focus: Georgia files 38.2 CFPB complaints per 1,000 residents — 2.4× the national rate of 15.9, and 22× Vermont's 1.7. The post-2019 explosion wasn't evenly national either: while the U.S. grew 19.6×, Mississippi grew 58.7×, Alabama 35.8×, Louisiana 31.3× — the credit-repair complaint wave has a regional center of gravity in the South.

  • Quietest states: Vermont 1.7, South Dakota 1.8, Montana 1.9 per 1,000 — the Georgia-to-Vermont spread is 22×.
  • Fastest growers since 2019 (states with ≥500 complaints then): MS 58.7×, AL 35.8×, LA 31.3×, TX 30.8×, AR 27.0× — though the surge wasn't purely Southern (Illinois grew 23.9×).
  • State buckets cover 99.85% of all 2025 complaints, so this isn't a sampling artifact.

Complaints are tagged by the consumer's self-reported state; population denominator is the July 2024 Census estimate (no 2025 vintage exists yet). Verified against live re-queries.

Cross-inference · CFPB × FDIC

Size-adjust the banks and the villain list flips upside down

Data: complaints 2023–2025 · FDIC deposits latest quarter · pulled 2026-08-21

Raw counts crowned Capital One the most-complained-about bank (71,613 complaints, 2023–2025). But banks aren't the same size. Divide each bank's complaints by its FDIC-reported deposits and the card issuers sweep the podium: Synchrony logs 360 complaints per $1B of deposits — nearly 20× PNC's 18 — while the megabanks everyone loves to hate land at the bottom of the table.

  • Rank flips: Synchrony #6 raw → #1 adjusted; Discover #7 → #2; JPMorgan #2 raw → #11 adjusted; Bank of America #4 raw → #10.
  • The three credit-card monolines take the top three; the four biggest full-service banks take four of the bottom five.
  • Every headline number was re-fetched live by the verifier — Synchrony's 30,915 complaints and $85.8B deposits reproduced exactly.

Honest limits: complaint buckets are holding-company-wide while deposits are one bank charter; card-focused banks fund with few deposits relative to their cardholder base, so "per deposit" partly measures business mix. Discover's charter is now inactive (merged into Capital One, 2025); its figures are the final pre-merger snapshot.

Cross-inference · NASA × human geography

The map of falling stars is really a map of who was watching

Data: full meteorite catalog, antiquity to 2013 vintage · pulled 2026-08-21

Split the meteorite catalog by how each rock entered it and you get two opposite planets. 86.6% of witnessed falls landed between 0° and 60°N — the band where most humans live. 71.1% of found meteorites sit below 60°S, in Antarctica, where nobody lives at all. Median latitude of a witnessed fall: +36° (roughly Nashville). Median latitude of a find: −73° (deep Antarctica). Same sky, same rain of rocks — sorted entirely by who was looking, and where searching works.

  • Northern Hemisphere share: 87.9% of falls vs just 25.1% of finds.
  • A "Fell" record needs an eyewitness; a "Found" record needs a searchable surface. Humans supply the first, Antarctic blue ice the second.
  • Caveat worth keeping: witnessed falls also skew toward regions with long written-record traditions, not raw population alone.

Cross-inference · NYPD × seasonal daylight

We tested whether darkness causes the 5 PM cyclist peak. It doesn't.

Data: crashes Jul 2012 – Aug 2026, winter vs summer · pulled 2026-08-21

A good hypothesis, killed by data — the fun kind. If darkness drove the evening injury peak, winter (sunset ~4:45 PM) should concentrate injuries into the dark 5–7 PM commute while summer spreads them across its long bright evenings. The seasons refuse to cooperate: both peak at exactly 18:00, winter's 5–7 PM share beats summer's by only 1.2 points (17.5% vs 16.3%), and the 7–10 PM shares are identical at 19.5%. The curves are near-twins despite summer logging 2.5× the injuries.

  • The 5–6 PM peak is commuter exposure: the hourly shape barely moves between a season dark at 4:45 PM and one light until 8:30 PM.
  • The verifier reproduced all 48 hourly bins exactly — and noted that because shares sum to 100%, summer's late-night riding mechanically depresses its evening shares, making the tiny "darkness effect" smaller still.
  • Fair caveat: winter riders are a hardier, commute-heavy population, so the test isn't perfectly clean — but a real darkness effect should still have shifted the winter curve, and it didn't.

Cross-inference · WA EV registry × Census income

EV adoption is an income story — r = 0.75 — except where it's a ferry story

Data: registry snapshot Aug 2026 · income 2023 · population 2024

Join Washington's 39 counties' EV counts to their median household incomes and the correlation is strong: Pearson r = 0.75 (income explains ~56% of the variance in EVs per 1,000 residents). Then the residuals get interesting: the #1 EV county isn't rich-tech King — it's San Juan, a mid-income archipelago where every gallon of gas arrives by ferry.

  • Top of the table: San Juan 72.6 EVs per 1,000 ($86k income), King 60.7 ($121k), Jefferson 47.7 ($78k). Bottom: Garfield 3.3 ($63k) — a 21.8× spread.
  • King County alone holds 141,971 of 293,432 WA-registered EVs — 48.4%. (The pull agent first wrote "45% of ~318k" from memory; the verifier caught it.)
  • The outliers argue income isn't destiny: San Juan over-adopts for its income; Whitman under-indexes partly because a college town's "low income" is a student artifact.

Dispatch · BTS · Border Crossing Entry Data, 1996–present

The border's busiest year was 1999

Data: Jan 1996 – Jul 2026 · pulled 2026-08-21

Every person, truck, bus, and train entering the U.S. by land since 1996, monthly, port by port — 275,901 rows through July 2026. The surprise isn't the pandemic dip. It's that people-crossings peaked at 387.7 million in 1999 and had already fallen 38% before COVID existed. Freight never got the memo: index both to 1996 and the lines tear apart.

  • In 2020, people-crossings collapsed 52%; trucks dipped 4.5%. Borders close to people, not to freight — 2024 set an all-time truck record (13.17M).
  • San Ysidro, CA has logged 1.04 billion people since 1996 — one San Diego crossing has admitted more people than the population of Europe.
  • Pedestrian crossings are a Mexican-border phenomenon almost entirely: 1.31 billion on foot from Mexico vs 14.6 million from Canada — 90:1.
  • 2024's people-crossings (227.8M) were back to 94% of 2019 — but still just 59% of the 1999 peak. The long decline started with 9/11-era documentation rules, not the pandemic.

Dispatch · NYC Open Data · Motor Vehicle Collisions, 2.27M crashes

Reported crashes fell by two-thirds. Deaths didn't fall at all.

Data: Jul 2012 – Aug 2026 · pulled 2026-08-21

New York City logs every police-reported crash — 2,269,187 since mid-2012. Reported crashes peaked at 231,564 in 2018 and have fallen every year since, to 85,546 in 2025. Deaths refused to follow: 231 people died in 2018, and 229 died in 2025. Divide one by the other and the line is blunt:

  • Cyclist injuries peak at 5 PM (5,498) — 12× the 4 AM floor (427), and more than double the 8 AM rush (2,574).
  • Cyclist injuries rose from 4,075 (2013) to 5,358 (2025) — up 31% while reported crashes fell by two-thirds. More people are riding; the streets haven't kept up.
  • The top named cause is no contest: Driver Inattention/Distraction, 462,105 crashes — 3.4× the runner-up, failure to yield.

"Reported crashes" is doing real work: NYPD largely stopped documenting minor no-injury crashes around 2020, which shrinks the denominator. Deaths are the hard number — and they are flat for a decade.

Dispatch · NASA · Meteorite Landings

Half of everything we've caught falling from space was picked up in Antarctica

Data: full catalog, 45,716 records to 2013 vintage · pulled 2026-08-21

45,716

documented meteorites — only 1,107 were seen falling

NASA's portal fought us for two rounds — the CSV endpoint hung, then threw SSL errors — before finally surrendering a 19 MB legacy XML export. Worth the fight: The Meteoritical Society's catalog of every known meteorite holds three quiet surprises about how humanity actually collects space rocks.

witnessed falls 1,107 · 2.4% found later 44,609 · 97.6% median mass 32.7 g mean mass 13.3 kg largest: Hoba 60 t
  • 94% of all known meteorites were recorded after 1970. Earth didn't start getting hit harder — scientists started sweeping Antarctic blue ice. 68.7% of validly located meteorites lie below 60°S (an earlier pass said 57.5%; junk rows at (0,0) were diluting the share — the verifiers caught it).
  • The mass distribution is absurdly skewed: median 32.7 grams, mean 13.3 kilograms — dragged 406× off the median by giants like Hoba, a 60-tonne slab never moved from the Namibian field where it was found in 1920.
  • Only 2.4% of meteorites were ever witnessed falling. Nearly everything we know about what hits us, we reconstructed after the fact.

Earth had 2,018 magnitude-5 earthquakes this year. That's normal.

Data: trailing 365 days, Aug 2025 – Aug 2026 · pulled 2026-08-21

The USGS event API answers in real time, so this story is a snapshot of the planet's trailing 365 days. The ladder is the lesson — each magnitude step up releases ~32× more energy and happens roughly a tenth as often:

M5+2,018about 5.5 per day, somewhere on Earth
M6+143one every two and a half days
M7+18one every three weeks
M8+0none this year — the top of the ladder stayed quiet
  • The year's largest: a M7.8 near Kablalan, Philippines (June 2026) and a M7.8 off Kamchatka (September 2025) — tied at the top.

  • The near-perfect ~10× thinning per magnitude step is the Gutenberg–Richter law, visible in a single API call: 2,018 → 143 → 18 → 0.

Dispatch · CFPB · Consumer Complaint Database

The federal complaint box has become a machine — and the machine is mostly one complaint

Data: Dec 2011 – Aug 2026 · pulled 2026-08-21

17,242,643

complaints in the public database at time of pull

The CFPB has published every consumer complaint it has handled since 2011. For a decade the volume grew steadily — around 250,000 a year by 2018. Then it went vertical: the database logged 5.44 million complaints dated 2025, and 2026 had already passed 4.56 million by late August. Complaint volume roughly doubled every year for three years straight.

  • One product category — credit reporting — was 88.4% of all 2025 complaints (4,810,303 of 5,442,974).
  • The three credit bureaus dwarf everyone: TransUnion 4.73M, Equifax 4.56M, Experian 4.13M — together 78% of every complaint ever filed. TransUnion has 25× more complaints than any actual bank.
  • Why? The CFPB itself says the portal is being flooded by AI-generated and duplicative complaints from credit-repair operations. The database quietly stopped measuring consumer anger and started measuring automation.

Public DB excludes complaints that fail publication criteria — CFPB's official 2025 count is higher (6.6M received, per its annual report). Trend shape is the same either way.

Dispatch · CDC/NCHS · Life expectancy at birth, 1900–2018

1918 erased eleven years of life expectancy in twelve months

Data: annual series 1900–2018 · pulled 2026-08-21

One tidy table, 119 years of American life. In 1917, a newborn could expect 50.9 years. In 1918 — influenza plus the war — that number collapsed to 39.1. It is the most violent single move in the series, and the rebound is just as strange: 1919 snapped back to 54.7, higher than before the pandemic, because the flu had already taken the most vulnerable.

  • The century's arc: 47.3 → 78.7, thirty-one years gained. The two halves differ in texture: jagged chaos before 1950, then an almost perfectly smooth climb — the switch from dying of infections to dying of chronic disease.
  • The last decade of the series is the quiet shock: 78.7 in 2010 → 78.7 in 2018. Zero net progress — the famous U.S. stagnation, before COVID ever arrived.
  • The female–male gap: just 1.0 year in 1920, peaking at 7.8 years in 1979, narrowing to 5.0 by 2018.

Dispatch · Washington State DOL · Electric Vehicle Population

Watching a technology arrive, one license plate at a time

Data: registry snapshot Aug 2026 · model years 1999–2027 · pulled 2026-08-21

Washington publishes a row for every EV currently registered in the state — 294,193 at time of pull. Because each row carries a model year, the registry doubles as a fossil record of adoption: a decade of polite growth, then a wall.

  • 41% of every EV in the state is a Tesla (120,899). Second place — Chevrolet — has 19,964.
  • Pure battery EVs outnumber plug-in hybrids 4 to 1. The transitional technology is losing to the destination.
  • Average range of the registered fleet 3.6×'d in a decade: 76 miles (2011 models) to 277 miles (2020 models).

One state, and a rich one — WA is not the national picture. Registrations lag and churn with the used market; model years run ahead of calendar years.

Dispatch · NYC Open Data · 2018 Central Park Squirrel Census

The government knows how many squirrels moaned at it

Data: October 2018 census · pulled 2026-08-21

In October 2018, volunteer sighters swept Central Park hectare by hectare and logged 3,023 squirrel encounters, each with coordinates, fur color, and a full behavioral readout. It is the most lovingly over-instrumented dataset in the catalog, and it's real, and it's on data.gov.

Gray 2,473 · 82% Cinnamon 392 · 13% Black 103 · 3.4% unrecorded 55
  • Central Park squirrels are thoroughly urbanized: 48% ignored the humans observing them, only 22% fled, and 5.9% walked right up — presumably invoicing for snacks.
  • It is a matter of public record that exactly three squirrels moaned in Central Park in October 2018.
  • The black-squirrel population — the internet's favorite — is real but rare: 1 in 29.
