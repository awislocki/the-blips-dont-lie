## The Docket

All 8 items below are House bills reported out of committee this week (version suffix `rh`) — none has passed either chamber yet. Six of the eight are part of what reads as a coordinated nuclear-regulatory-reform package moving through the Energy and Commerce Committee.

### CHILD Act of 2026 — BILLS-119hr3100rh

*(H.R. 3100; introduced Apr. 30, 2025 (as the underlying bill text, prior to this report's amendment); reported by House Judiciary Nov. 5, 2026. Full 6-page text read.)*

Amends the National Child Protection Act of 1993 to expand who a "qualified entity" (an organization working with children, the elderly, or the disabled) can request a background check on. Currently limited to direct employees and volunteers, the bill adds contractors, people employed by firms the entity contracts with, and anyone the entity licenses or certifies to provide care. Affects nonprofits, daycare operators, elder-care providers, and similar organizations, plus their contractors and licensees.

- **THE GOOD:** Closes a real coverage gap — contracted staff and licensed/certified providers have the same access to vulnerable people as direct employees but weren't previously eligible for a background check request under this statute. The amendment is narrow and textual, grafted onto an existing, well-established 1993 framework rather than creating a new scheme.
- **THE BAD:** The bill only grants organizations the *ability* to request these checks — it doesn't require them. An organization can still decline to check a contractor's background with no consequence. No funding or fee-offset provision accompanies the expanded eligibility, and background checks carry real processing costs.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a clean, single-purpose definitional expansion with no unrelated riders in the 6-page text.
- **Grade: B.** Sound, narrowly targeted fix; permissive rather than mandatory structure limits how much real-world protection it actually guarantees.

### Nuclear REFUEL Act — BILLS-119hr3978rh

*(H.R. 3978; introduced June 12, 2025; reported by House Energy & Commerce Nov. 5, 2026. Full 4-page text read.)*

Amends the Atomic Energy Act's definition of "production facility" to exclude equipment that reprocesses spent nuclear fuel *without* separating out pure plutonium (i.e., keeping plutonium mixed with other transuranic elements, a configuration generally considered more proliferation-resistant than isolated plutonium). That exclusion moves such equipment out of the AEA's heaviest licensing category. Affects nuclear fuel-recycling developers, DOE's advanced fuel-cycle programs, and the NRC.

- **THE GOOD:** Enables commercial development of proliferation-resistant spent-fuel recycling technology without subjecting it to the same "production facility" licensing tier used for uranium enrichment and weapons-relevant material — a defensible, narrowly drawn technical distinction.
- **THE BAD:** The 4-page text removes this equipment from "production facility" status but never states what licensing category *does* apply instead; a reader needs the broader Atomic Energy Act and NRC regulations to know what oversight remains. No safety or environmental analysis accompanies the text itself.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a single, clearly-scoped definitional carve-out.
- **Grade: B-.** Clear technical purpose, but incomplete on its own terms about what regulatory regime fills the gap it creates.

### Efficient Nuclear Licensing Hearings Act — BILLS-119hr5549rh

*(H.R. 5549; introduced Sept. 23, 2025; reported by House Energy & Commerce Nov. 5, 2026. Full 6-page text read.)*

Flips the default for NRC licensing hearings on nuclear facility construction and operating-license applications: instead of an automatic formal, on-the-record hearing, the NRC may issue a permit or license after 30 days' public notice unless an affected person specifically requests a hearing — and even then, using informal rather than formal adjudicatory procedures. The changes apply to all applications and proceedings pending on or after enactment. Affects nuclear facility applicants, the NRC, and anyone seeking to contest a nuclear license.

- **THE GOOD:** Targets a widely cited bottleneck — slow, automatic formal hearings — directly and transparently; it doesn't eliminate hearing rights, it makes them opt-in.
- **THE BAD:** Shifts the burden onto affected residents or advocacy groups to notice a Federal Register filing and affirmatively request a hearing within an unspecified window, rather than the NRC proactively convening one. It also governs uranium-enrichment-facility licensing hearings, extending the same opt-in model there.
- **THE UGLY:** The retroactive-applicability clause (Sec. 2(d)) sweeps in proceedings *already pending* when the bill is enacted — parties who entered a proceeding expecting an automatic hearing under the old rule could have that expectation pulled out from under them without any grandfathering.
- **Grade: C+.** Legitimate efficiency goal undercut by applying the new, harder-to-invoke process retroactively to matters already in motion.

### FRAUD in VA Disability Exams Act — BILLS-119hr5723rh

*(H.R. 5723; introduced Oct. 8, 2025; reported by House Veterans' Affairs Nov. 5, 2026. Full 10-page text read.)*

Requires the VA Secretary to stand up a fraud-detection process for disability benefit questionnaire (DBQ) forms — used to establish veterans' disability ratings — including a reporting pipeline to the VA Inspector General, routine audits, claimant notice when a DBQ is insufficient, and an annual report to Congress. Separately, it gives the VA Inspector General new authority to fund undercover investigations through dedicated accounts, with quarterly financial audits, annual reporting to six congressional committees, and a 3-year sunset on opening new such accounts. Takes effect 90 days after enactment. Affects veterans filing disability claims, VA claims processors, and the VA Office of Inspector General.

- **THE GOOD:** Targets a documented problem (fraudulent DBQs inflating disability ratings) with concrete mechanisms, and pairs new covert-investigation funding authority with real oversight: quarterly audits, a 3-year sunset on new accounts, and an extra-audit trigger above $50,000 in proceeds.
- **THE BAD:** The core fraud-detection mandate sets no deadline, no funding, and no specific audit frequency beyond "routinely" — implementation is left almost entirely to VA discretion. The claimant-notification duty tells someone their DBQ was "insufficient" but specifies no next step.
- **THE UGLY:** Section 3's undercover-investigation financial authority applies to *any* VA OIG undercover investigation, not just DBQ fraud — a scope broader than the "disability exams" short title suggests. It's disclosed plainly in the text rather than buried, and mirrors authority other inspectors general already hold, but it is scope creep relative to the bill's name.
- **Grade: B-.** Good target and real guardrails on the new investigative-funding authority; vague on-the-ground requirements for the fraud-detection mandate itself.

### American Enrichment Deployment Act — BILLS-119hr9612rh

*(H.R. 9612; introduced July 9, 2026; reported by House Energy & Commerce Nov. 5, 2026. Full 6-page text read.)*

Amends the Atomic Energy Act to let companies begin "at-risk" construction of uranium enrichment facilities before the NRC issues a license — the same pre-licensing construction option already available to other nuclear fuel-cycle facilities — with the company bearing the financial risk if the license is later denied. The NRC must issue or revise implementing regulations within 180 days. The bill explicitly preserves NEPA environmental review, third-party hearing rights, and NRC inspection authority. Affects uranium-enrichment developers, the NRC, and the domestic nuclear fuel supply chain.

- **THE GOOD:** Removes an enrichment-specific restriction that doesn't apply to other fuel-cycle facilities, aligning enrichment with an existing framework rather than inventing a new exemption. NEPA review, hearing rights, and inspection authority are explicitly preserved in the text, not left to inference.
- **THE BAD:** "At-risk construction" shifts financial risk to the company if licensure is later refused, but the bill specifies no wind-down or decommissioning requirement for a partially built, federally regulated nuclear materials facility in that scenario. The 180-day regulatory deadline carries no stated consequence if the NRC misses it.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a short, single-purpose bill with its safeguards stated plainly.
- **Grade: B.** Clean parity fix with explicit safeguards; the failed-license cleanup scenario is left unaddressed.

### Nuclear Advisory Committee Reform Act — BILLS-119hr9613rh

*(H.R. 9613; introduced July 9, 2026; reported by House Energy & Commerce Nov. 5, 2026. Full 8-page text read.)*

Restructures the Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards (ACRS), the independent panel that reviews nuclear reactor license applications for safety: caps membership at 15, imposes two 4-year term limits (with an exception the Commission can invoke for "compelling need"), and narrows ACRS's mandatory review focus to issues that are safety-significant, design-related, and "unique, novel, or noteworthy" — explicitly letting ACRS decline to review an application that doesn't meet that bar. Affects the NRC, ACRS members, and reactor-license applicants.

- **THE GOOD:** Term limits for a standing scientific advisory panel are a reasonable governance norm against entrenchment. Focusing mandatory review on genuinely novel or safety-significant issues could reduce time spent re-reviewing settled matters, potentially speeding licensing without obviously weakening scrutiny of first-of-kind designs.
- **THE BAD:** The "compelling need" exception to term limits is undefined and left entirely to the Commission's own judgment, with no disclosure requirement — a term limit that can be waived at will is a soft one.
- **THE UGLY:** The committee's new authority to "decline to review" an application it judges unremarkable hands that judgment to the committee itself, with no outside check or appeal mechanism specified in this text — a safety-review body deciding, unreviewed, when its own scrutiny isn't warranted.
- **Grade: C+.** Sensible governance tweaks paired with a self-granted exemption power that could quietly narrow independent safety review.

### NRC Staff Pay Alignment Act — BILLS-119hr9614rh

*(H.R. 9614; introduced July 9, 2026; reported by House Energy & Commerce Nov. 5, 2026. Full 8-page text read.)*

Lets the NRC Chairman set pay for certain senior "career appointee" technical staff up to 110% of the federal Senior Executive Service pay cap, applicable to current appointees regardless of when they were hired, without affecting the agency's SES performance-certification status. Builds on pay authority already created by the 2024 ADVANCE Act and adds a new reporting metric (count of staff whose pay was adjusted) to that law's existing GAO-reportable disclosures.

- **THE GOOD:** Targets an acknowledged federal recruiting problem — losing nuclear engineering and safety expertise to private-sector salaries — with a bounded cap (110%, not unlimited) and an added oversight-reporting line, building incrementally on an already-enacted framework rather than a parallel pay system.
- **THE BAD:** The text discloses no cost estimate and no count of affected positions, so a reader can't tell from the bill alone how much this adds to NRC's payroll. Retroactive applicability to existing appointees comes with no new performance or retention commitment attached.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a plainly scoped, single-purpose pay-authority amendment.
- **Grade: B-.** Reasonable retention tool with real reporting attached, docked for disclosing no cost figures.
