## The Docket

### Mystic Alerts Act — PLAW-119publ136

*(H.R. 7022; passed House Apr. 20, 2026, passed Senate Sept. 30, 2026; approved Oct. 22, 2026. Full 4-page text read.)*

Lets wireless carriers that already participate in the Wireless Emergency Alerts system choose whether to also relay emergency alerts via satellite to subscribers with satellite-capable phones. The FCC must issue a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking within 6 months and a final rule within 18 months of enactment setting the technical standards; the rule's effective date is the *later* of 36 months after its Federal Register publication or 12 months after DHS/FEMA finish their own supporting standards. No fee may be charged for the satellite-alert feature, subscribers can opt out, and participating carriers get a liability shield for sending (or failing to send) an alert and for sharing subscriber data with emergency responders in connection with one. Affects commercial mobile carriers offering satellite connectivity, their subscribers, the FCC, DHS, and FEMA.

- **THE GOOD:** Extends emergency alerting to satellite-connected phones, useful precisely where terrestrial cell coverage fails during a disaster. No-fee mandate and consumer opt-out are both explicit; FCC rulemaking has firm 6-month/18-month deadlines.
- **THE BAD:** Carrier participation is entirely voluntary — a carrier can opt out of satellite alerting altogether with nothing but a customer notice, so the Act doesn't guarantee anyone actually receives these alerts. Stacking the "later of" two multi-year clocks on the rule's effective date means real-world functionality could be five-plus years out.
- **THE UGLY:** The liability shield in Sec. 2(c)(1)(B) covers a carrier releasing subscriber information to law-enforcement officials, not just fire/EMS/emergency-management entities — a bit broader than the "emergency alert" framing implies, and worth flagging as scope creep into a liability waiver for law-enforcement data sharing.
- **Grade: C.** Sound goal undercut by voluntary participation and a liability shield that reaches further than its stated purpose.

### Southcentral Foundation Land Transfer Act of 2025 — PLAW-119publ130

*(H.R. 3620 / S. 2098; passed House Dec. 15, 2025, passed Senate Sept. 29, 2026; approved Oct. 20, 2026. Full 2-page text read.)*

Directs the HHS Secretary to convey roughly 3.372 acres of federal land in Anchorage, Alaska to the Southcentral Foundation (a tribal health nonprofit) by warranty deed within two years, at no cost, for use in health and social-services programs. Pre-existing environmental contamination liability stays with the government per CERCLA §120(h); the Foundation gets clear title with no reversionary interest retained by the U.S. Affects the Southcentral Foundation, HHS, and the Anchorage-area patients the Foundation serves.

- **THE GOOD:** Clean, narrowly scoped conveyance with a firm 2-year deadline, no cost to the recipient, and an environmental-liability carve-out that follows the standard CERCLA framework rather than inventing new terms.
- **THE BAD:** "No consideration" means the public effectively absorbs this land's market value with no appraisal figure or offsetting public benefit disclosed anywhere in the text.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a single-purpose conveyance that does only what its title says.
- **Grade: B.** Does exactly what it says cleanly; modest unquantified cost to taxpayers is the only ding.

### Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe Project Lands Restoration Act — PLAW-119publ128

*(H.R. 2388 / S. 1513; passed House Dec. 9, 2025, passed Senate Sept. 24, 2026; approved Oct. 20, 2026. Full 2-page text read.)*

Takes roughly 1,082.63 acres of federal land carved out of Olympic National Park into trust for the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, folding it into the Lower Elwha Reservation, and exempts the transfer from standard federal valuation/appraisal requirements. Explicitly bars the land from gaming use under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and preserves existing treaty rights and river-management obligations under the Elwha River Ecosystem Act and the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act. Affects the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe and the National Park Service/Interior Department.

- **THE GOOD:** The gaming prohibition and treaty-rights preservation clauses are explicit, not implied — both are common flashpoints in land-into-trust transfers, and this bill closes them off up front. River management continuity is handled by direct cross-reference to existing law rather than a new, untested scheme.
- **THE BAD:** Waiving "any requirements for valuation, appraisal, or equalization under any Federal law" is a broad exemption — there's no public accounting anywhere in the text of what roughly 1,083 acres of former national-park land is worth.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — narrow scope, and the carve-outs are stated plainly rather than buried.
- **Grade: B.** Well-targeted, with sensible guardrails; the blanket appraisal waiver is a shortcut worth naming even though it's not out of place for this kind of transfer.

### License to Drill Act — PLAW-119publ134

*(H.R. 7831 / S. 5039; passed House June 2, 2026, passed Senate Sept. 30, 2026; approved Oct. 20, 2026. Full 1-page text read.)*

Amends the Mineral Leasing Act to extend, from 2026 to 2037, both the period during which BLM collects a processing fee on new oil-and-gas drilling permit applications and the sunset date of the associated fund, and directs that all (not merely a specified share) of the fee revenue flow into the BLM Permit Processing Improvement Fund. Affects oil and gas operators applying for federal drilling permits and BLM's permit-processing operations.

- **THE GOOD:** A narrow, mechanical fix — extends two dates and redirects an existing fee stream entirely to the fund meant to speed up permit processing, which matches the fee's original stated purpose.
- **THE BAD:** The text discloses no dollar figures, no current permit backlog, and no performance benchmark tied to the 11-year extension — readers have to take "this speeds up permitting" on faith.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a one-page technical fee extension whose title matches its content exactly.
- **Grade: B-.** Clean technical fix; no accountability mechanism attached to whether the fund actually shortens permit turnaround.

### Clergy Act — PLAW-119publ125

*(H.R. 227; passed House Apr. 27, 2026, passed Senate Sept. 30, 2026; approved Oct. 20, 2026. Full 3-page text read.)*

Reopens, for ordained ministers, members of religious orders, and Christian Science practitioners who previously opted out of Social Security self-employment tax, a one-time window to revoke that exemption and opt back into coverage. The window runs through the due date of the filer's tax return for the second taxable year beginning after December 31, 2028; the revocation is irrevocable once filed, and claiming retroactive effect for a prior taxable year requires paying the full self-employment tax owed for that year. The IRS, with SSA, must submit an outreach plan to Congress within 90 days. Affects clergy and religious workers who elected the Sec. 1402(e)(1) exemption, plus IRS/SSA administration.

- **THE GOOD:** Gives clergy who opted out early in their careers — often before fully grasping the tradeoff — a genuine, one-time path back into Social Security coverage, paired with a mandatory outreach plan to Congress rather than a quietly published form.
- **THE BAD:** The window doesn't actually open until tax years after 2028 — more than two years after enactment — so nobody benefits immediately. Claiming retroactive credit requires paying the full back self-employment tax up front, a real barrier for lower-income religious workers.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found — a narrow technical fix to a decades-old, previously-addressed eligibility gap (similar windows existed around 1999–2002), not a surprise provision.
- **Grade: B-.** Solid incremental fix for a long-standing problem, blunted by a multi-year delay before anyone can use it.
