### Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 (`PLAW-119publ103`, signed 2026-09-02)

A four-division stopgap. **Division A** continues federal funding at FY2026 levels for all twelve regular appropriations bills through December 11, 2026 (or enactment of a real FY2027 bill for a given account, whichever comes first), with specific restrictions barring the Defense Department from starting new programs or increasing production rates during the stopgap. **Division B** extends roughly nineteen unrelated expiring authorities — among them the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015, the Federal Cybersecurity Enhancement Act, the Defense Production Act, AGOA, the Haiti Economic Lift Program, customs user fees, the Medicare Improvement Fund, and USPTO fee-setting authority. **Division C** (Surface Transportation Extension Act of 2026) extends highway and trust-fund program authority. **Division D** extends several VA health-care authorities (copayment collection, mandatory nursing-home care, related funding). Affects essentially every federal agency and contractor, highway and transit programs, veterans, and the dozens of narrower programs riding in Division B. *Text: opening, Division A's operative rules, and Section 2018 read in full; Divisions B, C, and D reviewed by section heading/title only (the packaged text runs to roughly 74,000 characters against this column's 60,000-character read cap).*

- **THE GOOD:** A hard, calendar-specific expiration (December 11, 2026) rather than an open-ended stopgap. Explicit anomaly language (Sec. 102) blocks the Pentagon from using stopgap funds to start new production or ramp up rates — a standard, sensible guardrail against backdoor program growth during a CR.
- **THE BAD:** By design, a stopgap gives no agency an actual FY2027 budget — planning uncertainty simply rolls forward another ten weeks. Section 2018 expressly removes the budgetary effects of Division B and "each succeeding division" from both the statutory and Senate PAYGO scorecards, meaning the cost of the extensions in Divisions B, C, and D is deliberately exempted from the normal pay-as-you-go accounting.
- **THE UGLY:** Bundling core government funding together with roughly nineteen unrelated program extensions and VA benefit rules into one must-pass vehicle is exactly the mechanism that forecloses a separate vote on any one of them — a member cannot oppose, say, the VA nursing-home mandate without also voting against keeping the government open. The PAYGO exemption is written in plain sight (not hidden), but its effect is to insulate the bill's real fiscal footprint from the mechanism built to track it.
- **Grade: C+.** A workmanlike, necessary stopgap; docked for the blanket PAYGO carve-out and for making a dozen unrelated riders un-opposable by attaching them to funding that must pass.

### Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 (`PLAW-119publ111`, signed 2026-09-18)

Division A imposes a sanctions regime on the Russian Federation — financial institutions, sovereign debt, energy exports, securities listings, uranium imports, and tariff surcharges on countries that buy Russian-origin oil or gas — and Title II extends the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996 and sets a sunset clause. Division B, unrelated to sanctions, expands the federal tax deduction for classroom supplies to cover early-childhood educators. Affects Russian- and Iranian-linked entities, U.S. financial institutions, importers/exporters trading with Russia, and early-childhood educators' tax returns. *Text: Section 1 (table of contents), Title I definitions, and Section 115 (Waiver) read in full; Division B and Sections 116–117 and 201–203 confirmed present by section heading/table-of-contents only, beyond this column's 60,000-character read cap on an 85,000-plus-character document.*

- **THE GOOD:** A specific, enumerated sanctions architecture (Secs. 102–113) naming financial, energy, securities, and trade channels, rather than a vague delegation. The waiver mechanism (Sec. 115) requires a written presidential certification to Congress before taking effect, not silent executive action.
- **THE BAD:** That same waiver lets the President waive "any sanctions provision" on certification and report alone — no vote, no congressional approval required. The tariff mechanism (Sec. 113) reaches third countries that buy Russian oil, not just Russia, which widens the bill's economic footprint well past its named target.
- **THE UGLY:** Division B — an early-childhood-educator tax deduction — has no substantive connection to Russia/Iran sanctions and rides on the back of an unrelated foreign-policy bill under a short title that gives no hint it's inside. Small in dollar terms, but a textbook rider.
- **Grade: B-.** Serious and specific on its stated subject; undercut by lightly-checked waiver discretion and an unrelated rider.

### Doug LaMalfa Federal Disaster Tax Relief Certainty Act (`PLAW-119publ108`, signed 2026-09-11)

Amends the Internal Revenue Code to make permanent (rather than case-by-case, disaster-by-disaster) the enhanced tax treatment of personal casualty losses from federally declared disasters and the exclusion from taxable income of wildfire-relief compensation payments. Applies to any federally declared major disaster with an incident period beginning after December 28, 2019 and before January 1, 2027. Affects individual taxpayers in disaster and wildfire-declared areas.

- **THE GOOD:** Converts a patchwork of one-off, retroactive relief bills Congress has passed after nearly every major disaster into a standing rule — taxpayers no longer need to wait for ad hoc legislation to know their casualty-loss deduction or wildfire-payment exclusion applies. Available to non-itemizers too (Sec. 2(c)), broadening its practical reach.
- **THE BAD:** The relief is not actually permanent — it's bounded to disasters declared before January 1, 2027, so this "certainty" act itself has an expiration date that will need revisiting. The rule change is retroactively effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2024, which affects the current filing calculations for wildfire and disaster victims from the past two tax years.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the scope, dates, and coordination with prior relief statutes are stated explicitly in the text (Sec. 2(d)(2)).
- **Grade: B.** Solid, narrowly-targeted tax cleanup; the short title's "Certainty" promise is somewhat oversold given the built-in 2027 cutoff.

### Stop Secret Spending Act of 2025 (`PLAW-119publ106`, signed 2026-09-11)

Amends the Federal Funding Accountability and Transparency Act of 2006 to require that "other transaction agreements" — a category of federal contracts historically exempt from standard procurement disclosure — be reported to USAspending.gov within three years, with an annual report on any federal spending still not publicly posted and the reasons why (national security, classified, legislative/judicial branch, or sub-award status). Also directs a GAO report on updating the relevant Federal Acquisition Regulation clause. Affects federal agencies with OTA authority (notably Defense and other research-heavy agencies) and public spending-transparency advocates.

- **THE GOOD:** Closes a genuine, well-known transparency gap — other transaction agreements have grown rapidly in dollar volume specifically because they sidestep normal contract disclosure, and this requires them onto the public spending database. The annual "why wasn't this posted" report (Sec. 2(c)) creates an accountability trail even for legitimately withheld spending.
- **THE BAD:** Three full years before OTA data must be automatically flowing to USAspending.gov, plus up to two more years of phase-in reporting if the deadline is missed — a five-year runway before full compliance is guaranteed. No penalty attaches to an agency that simply keeps missing the deadlines described in the phase-in report requirement.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the bill does exactly what its short title claims, on the timeline it states.
- **Grade: B.** A real transparency fix, weakened only by a long implementation runway with no enforcement teeth.

### Prison Staff Safety Enhancement Act (`PLAW-119publ110`, signed 2026-09-16)

Responds to a 2023 DOJ Inspector General report finding the Bureau of Prisons inadequately tracks and mitigates inmate-on-staff sexual harassment and assault. Requires the Bureau to implement the IG's recommendations within 90 days (or report to Congress on why not and when), requires a follow-up IG data analysis roughly a year after full implementation, and directs the Attorney General to issue national standards for prevention and punishment of such incidents. Affects Bureau of Prisons correctional staff and incarcerated individuals.

- **THE GOOD:** Built directly on a documented, specific IG finding rather than a generic mandate — the Bureau either implements the named recommendations or has to explain itself to Congress on a fixed 90-day clock. Requires an analysis of actual punishment data going back five years, forcing a look at whether existing penalties have been used at all.
- **THE BAD:** No consequence is specified if the Bureau reports it hasn't implemented the recommendations — the "report explaining the failure" (Sec. 3(b)(2)) is itself the only enforcement mechanism. The Attorney General's rulemaking deadline is calculated from the IG's follow-up analysis, which itself depends on the Bureau first finishing implementation — a chain of sequential deadlines that could stretch several years before national standards exist.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the bill's scope matches its title, and it names the underlying IG report directly rather than working around it.
- **Grade: B-.** A clear-eyed response to a documented problem, undercut by a compliance mechanism that's just another report.

### Local Access to Courts Act / LACA (`PLAW-119publ109`, signed 2026-09-16)

A one-page, two-section technical fix: adds College Station, Texas and El Centro, California to the list of cities where the relevant U.S. District Courts (Southern District of Texas and Southern District of California, respectively) must hold sessions. Affects litigants and attorneys in those two metro areas who currently must travel further to appear in federal court.

- **THE GOOD:** A concrete, minor access-to-justice improvement — real people in two specific places get a closer federal courthouse venue, and the bill states exactly which statute sections are amended and how.
- **THE BAD:** Purely geographic; it does nothing about court staffing, judge vacancies, or case backlogs, which are the more common drivers of "access to courts" problems nationally.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — a two-line technical amendment doing exactly what it says.
- **Grade: B.** Modest and uncontroversial; a plumbing fix, not a reform.

### Paducah, Kentucky land-parcel deed restriction removal (`PLAW-119publ105`, signed 2026-09-11)

Directs the Secretary of the Interior to remove deed restrictions from a roughly 3.6-acre parcel in Paducah, Kentucky (the former Paducah Memorial Army Reserve Center), conveyed to the city in 2012. The city may not sell the land to anyone but the Oscar Cross Boys & Girls Club of Paducah; if it does, that club must first offer the land back to the federal government for free before selling to anyone else; any future use must stay compatible with public or recreational purposes. Affects the city of Paducah and the named Boys & Girls Club chapter.

- **THE GOOD:** The conditions attached to the restriction's removal are specific and protective — a single named beneficiary, a right-of-first-refusal back to the federal government, and a durable public-use requirement — rather than an unconditional handoff.
- **THE BAD:** It is a genuinely single-purpose, single-parcel private bill; it sets no precedent or general standard, and required a full act of Congress to resolve what is, functionally, a local property dispute.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the beneficiary, conditions, and exact parcel are named plainly in the text, not obscured.
- **Grade: C+.** Honest, narrow, and exactly as small as it appears — the legislative equivalent of a rounding error.
