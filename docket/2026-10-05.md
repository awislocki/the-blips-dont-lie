### Miami Tribe of Oklahoma / Illinois land claim settlement (`PLAW-119publ114`, signed 2026-09-25)

Grants the U.S. Court of Federal Claims jurisdiction to hear a specific land claim by the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma under the 1805 Treaty of Grouseland, overriding the normal statute of limitations and any delay-based defense — but only if the Tribe files within one year of enactment. Every other past, present, and future Miami Tribe land claim to Illinois land is permanently extinguished except that one preserved claim. Affects the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma and current landowners/title-holders in the affected Illinois claim area. *Text: read in full (2,673 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** A clean, final mechanism — it gives the Tribe one clear path to litigate its claim on the merits without the government raising a time-bar defense, while giving current Illinois landowners certainty that no other claim can surface later.
- **THE BAD:** A one-year filing window is a tight clock for deciding whether and how to litigate a claim rooted in an 1805 treaty. The bill takes no position on the claim's merits — it only opens, and then permanently closes, a courthouse door.
- **THE UGLY:** The extinguishment clause reaches not just the Tribe but any "member, descendant, or predecessor in interest" — a sweeping scope for a bill billed as a narrow settlement. This is a standard structure in land-claims statutes, not a hidden trick, but it's broad all the same.
- **Grade: B.** A standard-form land-claims bill, clearly drafted for exactly what it does.

### Hydropower Licensing Transparency Act (`PLAW-119publ112`, signed 2026-09-25)

Amends the Federal Power Act to require FERC to submit an annual report to Congress on the status of hydropower relicensing applications — new licenses, subsequent licenses, and original licenses — where a party notified FERC of intent to file at least three years earlier but no license has yet issued. Each report must list filing dates, docket numbers, application status, anticipated issuance timing, upcoming proceedings, and any pending actions, broken out by license type. Affects FERC, utilities and dam operators seeking relicensing, and the states, tribes, and municipalities involved in those proceedings. *Text: read in full (4,691 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** A real, recurring reporting requirement with concrete mandated contents — not a vague "report on progress," but specific docket numbers, dates, and disaggregation by license type. It targets only genuinely stalled multi-year backlogs (3+ years pending), a well-documented bottleneck.
- **THE BAD:** It's purely informational. There's no deadline, process reform, or consequence attached to the delays it exposes — FERC now has to report on its own slowness but faces no new pressure to fix it. The first report isn't due for 180 days after enactment.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — narrow, single-subject, and does exactly what its short title says.
- **Grade: B-.** A genuine sunshine measure with no teeth attached to the delays it illuminates.

### Fort Peck Reservation Rural Water System Act reauthorization (`PLAW-119publ113`, signed 2026-09-25)

A one-sentence amendment extending the authorization window for the Fort Peck Reservation Rural Water System Act of 2000 — previously set to expire in 2026 — to 2028, in both the construction and funding subsections. Affects the Fort Peck Reservation (Montana) rural water system's federal construction-funding authority. *Text: read in full (1,158 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** Exactly as advertised — a minimal extension that keeps an already-authorized rural water system project from losing its construction authority mid-build.
- **THE BAD:** A pure two-year kick-the-can. No new funding, oversight, or completion requirement comes with it, so Congress will likely be back here again before 2028.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found.
- **Grade: C+.** Housekeeping, competently executed.

### Romance Scam Prevention Act (`BILLS-119hr2481enr`, passed, enrolled 2026-09-29)

Requires online dating service providers to notify a member, within 24 hours (or up to 3 days in some cases), if that member has exchanged messages with another member whose account was later banned for suspected fraud. Notifications must identify the banned account, warn against sending money or financial information, and point to anti-fraud resources; enforcement runs through the FTC and state attorneys general, with a one-year delayed effective date. Affects every U.S. user of a commercial online dating platform and the platforms themselves. *Text: read in full (12,814 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** A specific, consumer-protection-forward rule with real deadlines, required notification content, and dual federal/state enforcement — a direct response to a well-documented fraud pattern (romance scams), not a vague directive.
- **THE BAD:** The statute lets each platform decide, "in the judgment of the ... provider," what counts as a fraud ban that triggers the notice — there's no external audit of ban decisions, and the rule only helps after a platform has already caught and banned someone. The one-year delayed effective date pushes real-world protection well into the future.
- **THE UGLY:** Section 2(c) sets "one national standard" and bars states from writing their own, different, or stronger dating-fraud notification rules — a federal preemption clause riding inside what otherwise reads as a narrowly scoped consumer-protection bill.
- **Grade: B.** A genuinely useful anti-fraud disclosure rule, undercut by self-judged triggering and a quiet preemption rider.

### Terrorism Risk Insurance Program Reauthorization Act of 2026 (`BILLS-119s4395es`, passed Senate 2026-09-28)

Extends the federal terrorism-risk-insurance backstop (TRIA) from its scheduled 2027 expiration to 2034 — a seven-year extension — and shifts related recoupment-timing deadlines by the same seven years to match. Affects the insurance industry and any commercial policyholder relying on federally backstopped terrorism coverage. *Text: read in full (2,295 characters); passed the Senate, not yet through the House as of this report.*

- **THE GOOD:** Removes near-term uncertainty for insurers and commercial policyholders who depend on the backstop remaining in place, and does so with a long runway (seven years) rather than another short-term patch.
- **THE BAD:** Pure date-shifting — no change to loss thresholds, industry retention levels, taxpayer exposure caps, or program structure. Congress will revisit the identical questions in 2034 rather than having fixed anything about the program's design now.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — a one-section bill that does only what its short title says.
- **Grade: B-.** Necessary continuity, zero substantive reform.

### Critical Infrastructure Manufacturing Feasibility Act (`BILLS-119hr1721enr`, passed, enrolled 2026-09-29)

Directs the Secretary of Commerce to study, within 18 months, which products in the 16 federally designated critical-infrastructure sectors are currently imported due to U.S. manufacturing or supply-chain constraints, and whether domestic manufacturing of those products — including in rural areas or industrial parks — is feasible, with a public report to Congress. Affects the Commerce Department and, indirectly, domestic manufacturers in critical-infrastructure supply chains. *Text: read in full (3,267 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** Requires a specific, structured analysis — job and cost effects, rural/industrial-park feasibility, sector-by-sector breakdown — rather than an open-ended mandate to "look into reshoring."
- **THE BAD:** The Secretary has no authority to compel data from private industry (Sec. 2(c)), so the study's quality depends entirely on voluntary cooperation from the companies it's studying. It produces a report, not funding, incentives, or any actual reshoring policy.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — modest in scope and honestly framed as a study, not an action bill, despite the "critical infrastructure" framing in its title.
- **Grade: C+.** A feasibility study with no power to gather the feasibility data it needs.

### Vicksburg National Military Park Boundary Modification Act (`BILLS-119hr4467enr`, passed, enrolled 2026-09-29)

Directs the Secretary of the Interior to convey roughly 7.4 acres of federal land inside Vicksburg National Military Park's boundary to the State of Mississippi, by quitclaim deed and without payment, and to shrink the Park's official boundary to exclude that land. The conveyed land may be used only to improve public access and visitor experience at the Park, may not harm Park resources, and remains subject to Secretary-imposed use restrictions. Affects the National Park Service and the State of Mississippi. *Text: read in full (3,150 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** The conveyance is tightly conditioned — restricted purpose (visitor access/experience), an explicit no-harm-to-Park clause, and continuing federal use restrictions — rather than an unconditional handoff of protected parkland.
- **THE BAD:** It permanently removes acreage from a protected national military park's jurisdiction, a precedent of park boundaries shrinking rather than growing, even in service of a stated public-access purpose.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — exact acreage and maps are cited specifically, and the purpose is stated plainly.
- **Grade: B-.** A narrow, well-documented park-boundary trade.

### Reversionary Interest Conveyance Act (`BILLS-119hr952enr`, passed, enrolled 2026-09-29)

Directs the Secretary of the Interior, through the Bureau of Land Management, to convey the United States' reversionary interest in roughly 8.43 acres of land in Sacramento, California, to the current owners of record, for not less than fair market value as determined under standard federal appraisal rules, with the buyer covering all transaction costs. Proceeds go to the Federal Land Disposal Account. Affects current owners of the affected Sacramento parcels and the Bureau of Land Management. *Text: read in full (4,603 characters).*

- **THE GOOD:** Requires payment of fair market value rather than a giveaway, and limits eligible buyers to current owners of record of the specific parcels — a clean, self-funding title cleanup.
- **THE BAD:** The Secretary gets up to two years after a buyer's request to complete the conveyance — a notably long compliance window for what is, functionally, a routine real-estate transaction.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — a narrow, single-purpose land-title cleanup matching its short title exactly.
- **Grade: B-.** A straightforward, paid cleanup of old federal reversionary rights.
