## The Docket

### American Music Tourism Act of 2025 — PLAW-119publ121

Amends the Visit America Act to direct Commerce's Assistant Secretary for Travel and Tourism to identify U.S. locations and events significant to "music tourism" and promote both domestic and international travel to them. Defines music tourism broadly (museums, studios, venues, festivals, concerts, live performances). Requires a report to the Senate Commerce Committee and House Energy and Commerce Committee within one year of enactment, then every two years. Affects the Commerce Department's existing tourism-promotion office and, indirectly, the music and travel industries.

- **THE GOOD:** A narrow, low-risk addition to an existing statutory framework rather than a new program; builds in recurring congressional reporting so the activity doesn't just happen quietly.
- **THE BAD:** No funding is authorized or appropriated, no metrics of success are specified, and "promote" is never defined with any enforceable content — the law could be satisfied with a webpage.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing buried found in the 2-page text; a small bill that does exactly what its short title says.
- **Grade: C.** Harmless and essentially cost-free, which is also the most interesting thing about it.

### Billion Dollar Boondoggle Act of 2025 — PLAW-119publ120

Requires OMB to issue guidance compelling federal agencies to report annually on "covered projects" — those more than five years behind their original completion date, or at least $1 billion over their original cost estimate — including cost history, schedule slippage, contractors, and the agency's own stated reasons for the overrun. OMB must compile and publicly post the results. Affects every executive and independent regulatory agency running major acquisitions, construction, or procurement programs.

- **THE GOOD:** A genuine transparency mechanism. It standardizes disclosure of major federal cost and schedule overruns across the entire government and forces the results onto a public OMB page, giving watchdogs, GAO, and reporters a dataset that doesn't currently exist in this form.
- **THE BAD:** The thresholds ($1B over budget, or 5 years late) are high bars that exclude plenty of smaller-but-still-wasteful projects. The law has no penalty provision for an agency that under-reports, mischaracterizes delays, or simply misses the deadline. The "reasons for delays" field is self-reported by the same agency that caused them.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing uncovered beyond the enforcement gap noted above — the text is a straightforward reporting mandate.
- **Grade: B.** Cleanly drafted and genuinely useful if OMB's guidance (due within a year) has any teeth; the law can't make that guidance strong, it can only require that it exist.

### Homebuyers Privacy Protection Act — PLAW-119publ36

Amends the Fair Credit Reporting Act to restrict "trigger leads": the practice of credit bureaus selling a mortgage applicant's credit-pull data to lenders who then solicit that consumer directly. Under the new rule, a bureau generally can't furnish such a report to another lender unless it's a firm credit offer from a party with an existing relationship to the consumer — their current loan originator, their servicer, or a depository institution already holding their account. Requires a GAO study within 12 months on trigger leads delivered by text message. Takes effect 180 days after enactment. Affects mortgage applicants, credit bureaus, and lenders who buy trigger-lead data.

- **THE GOOD:** Targets a specific, well-documented consumer harm — the barrage of unsolicited calls and texts that follows a mortgage credit pull — with a narrow, targeted FCRA amendment rather than a sweeping rewrite.
- **THE BAD:** The 180-day delayed effective date gives the current trigger-lead industry a half-year runway to keep operating unchanged. The carve-outs (existing servicer, originator, or depository relationship) are broad enough that large banks with existing retail footprints largely keep their marketing access; it's really only outside lead-buyers who get cut off.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing found in the operative text.
- **Grade: B+.** Tightly scoped and aimed at a real complaint; loses a notch for the long phase-in.

### Cybersecurity for Small Businesses Act of 2026 — BILLS-119hr10238rh

*Analysis note: this bill has not passed either chamber. It is the only package this week that advanced past introduction — reported by the House Committee on Small Business on 2026-10-13 (Report No. 119-851) — so it fills the Docket's bill slot under this column's significance ladder.*

Directs the SBA Administrator, in consultation with CISA and NIST, to develop and disseminate cybersecurity information and best practices for small businesses. Separately directs SBA, working with the Department of Defense, to inform small businesses seeking federal contracts about the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) program and related DoD small-business resources, and to consult annually with the SBA's Chief Counsel for Advocacy on best practices for sharing that information. Requires an annual report to Congress, starting 90 days after enactment, on the volume and nature of small-business cybersecurity inquiries received. Affects small businesses generally and small federal contractors/subcontractors specifically; implementing agencies are SBA, DoD, CISA, and NIST.

- **THE GOOD:** Targets a real gap — small businesses are frequent cyberattack targets but typically lack the staff to parse CISA/NIST guidance or Pentagon contracting paperwork like CMMC on their own. Routing outreach through SBA with required cross-agency consultation is a sensible, low-cost fix, and the annual reporting requirement creates a paper trail for oversight.
- **THE BAD:** This is purely an information and outreach mandate: no funding is authorized, there's no enforcement mechanism, and "develop and disseminate information" could be satisfied by a webpage and a mailing list. Success is measured only by how many inquiries get logged, not by any security outcome.
- **THE UGLY:** None found in the 6 pages reviewed — no definitions section, no funding language, no penalties, and no scope creep beyond the stated purpose.
- **Grade: C+.** Sound intent, toothless execution — likely to produce a brochure rather than a capability, though a comparatively well-contained one.
