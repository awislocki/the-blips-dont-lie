### Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 (`BILLS-119hr5334enr`)

Packaged as two divisions in one enrolled bill. Division A imposes a broad sanctions regime on the Russian Federation — financial institutions, sovereign debt, energy exports, securities listings, import bans, and tariff surcharges on countries that buy Russian-origin oil — and extends the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996. Division B, unrelated to sanctions, expands the federal tax deduction for classroom supplies to cover early-childhood educators, not just K-12 teachers. Affects Russian and Iranian-linked entities, U.S. financial institutions, importers/exporters trading with Russia, and (via Division B) early-childhood educators' tax returns.

- **THE GOOD:** A comprehensive, specific sanctions architecture (Sections 102–113) rather than vague delegated authority — it names financial, energy, securities, and trade channels explicitly. A hard 5-year sunset (Sec. 203) prevents the sanctions regime from running indefinitely without renewed congressional action. Waivers and terminations both require a written presidential certification and a report to Congress before they take effect (Secs. 115, 117).
- **THE BAD:** The waiver authority in Section 115 lets the President waive "any sanctions provision" on notice to Congress alone — no vote or approval required, just a report. Termination of Russia-specific sanctions is conditioned on Russia signing a peace agreement "accepted by the free and independent Government of Ukraine" (Sec. 117(a)(1)(A)) — a standard with real political judgment calls baked in and no further definition in the text. The oil-tariff mechanism (Sec. 113) reaches third countries, not just Russia, which broadens the bill's economic footprint beyond its stated target.
- **THE UGLY:** Division B — the early-childhood-educator tax deduction — has no substantive connection to Russia/Iran sanctions and is bundled onto the back of an unrelated foreign-policy bill. It's a small provision, but it's the textbook definition of a legislative rider: a policy change riding on a bill whose short title gives no hint it's inside.
- **Grade: B-.** Substantively serious on its stated subject, competently drafted, undercut by the unrelated rider and by handing broad, lightly-checked waiver discretion to the executive.

### Modernizing Access to Our Public Oceans Act (`BILLS-119s759enr`)

Directs the Secretary of Commerce, through NOAA, to standardize and publish geospatial data on fishing restrictions, marine protected areas, and recreational-vessel access in the U.S. exclusive economic zone, with a public website, defined update cadences, and privacy carve-outs. Affects recreational boaters, commercial fishers, Tribes with treaty fishing rights, and NOAA itself.

- **THE GOOD:** Concrete deadlines (31 months for data standards, 4 years for the public website) and defined update frequencies (at least twice yearly, some data in real time). Explicit nondisclosure protections for archaeological/cultural site locations and proprietary commercial fishing data (Sec. 4(f)). A rule-of-construction section (Sec. 6) expressly preserves existing tribal treaty rights and leaves agency jurisdiction and the legal definition of "navigable waters" untouched.
- **THE BAD:** Nearly every obligation is qualified by "to the maximum extent practicable," which softens hard deadlines into best-effort targets. Up to four years pass before the public sees any data. No appropriation or cost figure appears anywhere in the text.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the bill's scope matches its short title, and its carve-outs are stated plainly rather than buried.
- **Grade: B.** A sensible transparency mandate weakened by soft-deadline language that leaves enforcement, and timing, uncertain.

### Billion Dollar Boondoggle Act of 2025 (`BILLS-119s766enr`)

Requires the Director of the Office of Management and Budget to collect and publish, annually, detailed cost and schedule data for any federal project — civilian or defense — that is more than 5 years behind schedule or at least $1 billion over its original cost estimate. Affects federal contracting agencies, their contractors, and OMB's reporting workload.

- **THE GOOD:** An objective, quantifiable trigger ($1B over cost or 5 years late) rather than discretionary agency judgment about what counts as a "boondoggle." Requires public posting on OMB's website, not just an internal memo. Covers both civilian procurement and major defense acquisition programs under one standard.
- **THE BAD:** OMB has a full year just to issue implementing guidance before agencies must start reporting, so the first public report is years away. The bill creates a disclosure requirement with no penalty or corrective-action mechanism attached — it names the boondoggle, but doesn't require anyone to fix it. The $1B threshold means a $900 million overrun escapes the reporting requirement entirely.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found in the text. The short title's "Boondoggle" framing is more colorful than the underlying mechanism, which is a straightforward reporting mandate — a labeling choice, not a hidden provision.
- **Grade: B.** A reasonable accountability tool for the biggest cost overruns; slow to start and has no teeth once it starts.

### Indian land dispute settlement, Miami Tribe of Oklahoma (`BILLS-119s550enr`)

Grants the U.S. Court of Federal Claims jurisdiction — waiving the normal statute of limitations — to hear a specific land claim by the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma under an 1805 treaty covering land in Illinois, with a one-year window to file. Once that window closes, all other and future claims by the Tribe to Illinois land are permanently extinguished. Affects the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma and any current holders of the disputed Illinois land.

- **THE GOOD:** Gives the Tribe a genuine legal avenue to litigate a 220-year-old treaty claim that would otherwise be time-barred, with a firm one-year deadline that forces resolution rather than leaving the matter in permanent limbo.
- **THE BAD:** Extremely narrow — a single tribe, a single state, a single treaty — so it sets no general standard other tribes with similar claims could invoke. The extinguishment of "any and all future claims" (Sec. 1(b)) is a real, permanent forfeiture the Tribe accepts as the price of getting this one claim heard.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — the trade-off (one claim heard, all others extinguished) is stated in two adjacent, plainly worded sections, not buried.
- **Grade: B-.** Legally consequential and honestly drafted, but readers should note what's being given up in exchange for what's being granted.

### Kay Hagan Tick Reauthorization Act (`BILLS-119s2398enr`)

Reauthorizes existing CDC/HHS vector-borne-disease programs — the National Strategy and Regional Centers of Excellence, and health-department support grants — through fiscal year 2030. The prior authorization covered FY2021–2025, meaning it had already lapsed for most of 2026 by the time this passed. Affects public-health agencies and the CDC's tick-borne disease programs.

- **THE GOOD:** Keeps established disease-surveillance infrastructure running rather than letting it wind down; a minor structural tweak lets the program act "through" appropriate individuals rather than a since-defunct dedicated working group.
- **THE BAD:** The reauthorization arrives after the prior five-year authorization had already expired, meaning there was a gap. The text sets no funding levels — those live in separate appropriations bills, so this text alone can't confirm continuity of actual dollars.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found — a one-page, plain reauthorization.
- **Grade: B.** Unglamorous maintenance legislation, docked slightly for renewing the program only after its authorization had already lapsed.

### Hydropower Licensing Transparency Act (`BILLS-119hr3657enr`)

Requires the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to report annually to Congress on the status of pending hydropower relicensing applications, broken out by license type. Affects FERC, existing hydropower licensees, and parties (states, Tribes, municipalities) seeking new licenses.

- **THE GOOD:** A narrow, low-cost transparency requirement — no new substantive authority created, just a structured annual accounting of where relicensing applications stand, disaggregated by license type for usability.
- **THE BAD:** No enforcement mechanism if FERC misses the reporting deadline. The bill documents relicensing delays without doing anything to address their underlying causes.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found.
- **Grade: B.** Does exactly what its short title says, nothing more.

### American Music Tourism Act of 2025 (`BILLS-119s195enr`)

Amends the Visit America Act to add "music tourism" to the domestic and international promotion duties of the Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Travel and Tourism, and requires a report to two congressional committees on progress every two years. Affects the Commerce Department's tourism-promotion office.

- **THE GOOD:** Folds a new priority into an existing statutory office rather than creating new bureaucracy, and attaches a recurring reporting requirement so the added duty is at least nominally tracked.
- **THE BAD:** No funding is authorized or appropriated for the new duty — the office must absorb music-tourism promotion within its existing budget. "Important to music tourism" is left undefined, giving the Assistant Secretary broad discretion over what qualifies.
- **THE UGLY:** Nothing ugly found.
- **Grade: C+.** Modest boosterism with a reporting requirement attached; low cost, low stakes, correspondingly low ambition.
