The Mississippi File

One state moving in two directions at once. Mississippi's classrooms staged the most famous comeback in American education while its lifespans fell to last in the nation — and the drawers of the federal card file hold both trajectories, year by year, going back three decades.

The verdict, in two columns

Progress and decline, side by side

▲ Rising: the classroomGrade-4 reading: 49th of 50 states in 2013 → 9th in 2024, gaining 10.0 points while the nation lost 6.8. The lines fully crossed: MS now reads 3.5 points above the national average.
▲ Rising: fewer teen mothersTeen births fell 66% from the 1991 peak (85.3 → 29.1 per 1,000) — still 2nd-highest nationally, but the collapse is real and steep.
▲ Holding: poverty edging down21.4% (1995) → 18.3% (2023), though it detoured through a 22.4% peak in 2010 and remains ~6 points above the U.S.
▼ Falling: the horizonLife expectancy 74.6 (2018) → 70.9 (2021) — last in the nation three years running, with the MS–US gap widening from 4.1 to 5.1 years.
▼ Falling: the paycheck gapMedian income was 77.8% of the U.S. level in 1995; 70.0% in 2023. Nominal income doubled — and lost ground the whole time.
▼ Falling: the headcountPopulation peaked at the 2010 Census and has shrunk since (−24,252); the IRS measures a steady drip of departing income (−$66M AGI in 2022–23).
■ Stuck: the waistlineAdult obesity has hovered at 39–41% since 2018 (40.4% in 2024, 2nd only to West Virginia), about 7 points above the nation.

Why the horizon fell · NCHS leading causes × VSRR overdose surveillance

It's the hearts, not the opioids

Data: heart disease age-adjusted mortality 1999–2017 · overdose deaths 2015–2025 provisional

Decompose the life-expectancy deficit and the usual suspect is innocent: Mississippi's drug-overdose death rate runs about a third below the national rate (roughly 21.5 vs 31.9 per 100k in 2023) and has fallen faster since the 2021 peak (−39% vs −34%). The killer is chronic: heart-disease mortality 40% above the nation — Mississippi's 2017 rate (231.6 per 100k, age-adjusted) is the rate the United States last had in 2003. The state's hearts run fourteen years behind.

MS overdose deaths 746 (2021) → 453 (2025) US overdose 107,573 → 70,637 MS heart 2017 231.6 = US 2003 obesity +7 pts vs nation (above)

Overdose counts are VSRR 12-month-ending provisional figures (recent months still completing); per-capita comparison computed against 2023–24 populations. Heart-disease rates are age-adjusted, so this is not an age-structure artifact.

Why the headcount falls · Census components of change, 2011–2024

The population engine went into reverse in 2021

Data: births, deaths, and migration components · vintage-2019 and vintage-2024 estimates

Population change has exactly four moving parts, and Census publishes all of them. In 2011, Mississippi's births out-numbered deaths by +10,569. Births then slid every single year (39,734 → 33,859) while deaths climbed — and in 2021 the lines crossed: 41,641 deaths against 35,004 births, the state's first natural decrease. It has stayed negative ever since. Add the second engine — domestic migration, negative in all fourteen years (worst: −14,775 in 2018) — and the only positive line left is international arrivals, which jumped to ~6–7k a year after 2022 and now nearly carry the state alone.

Components are Census estimates (vintage 2019 for 2011–2019, vintage 2024 for 2021–2024; the vintages join with a gap at 2020). Domestic migration counts residents, not income — the IRS AGI drain above is its dollar shadow.

Why the averages mislead · SAIPE 2023, all 82 counties

There are two Mississippis

Data: median household income and poverty by county · 2023

The state's averages hide a split the county file makes brutal. Madison County ($82,057) and DeSoto County ($81,956) both earn more than the U.S. median household ($77,719) — suburban Jackson and suburban Memphis are national-average America with better barbecue. Ninety minutes away, Delta counties live in a different economy: Claiborne at $28,579, Holmes at $30,542 with 35.6% poverty, and Issaquena — the state's emptiest county — at 49.6% poverty. Top to bottom, the income spread inside one state is 2.9×.

  • The rising Mississippi (classrooms, suburbs, ports) and the declining one (Delta, lifespans) are largely different places — statewide trend lines average two divergent states.
  • Poverty runs from 9.2% (DeSoto) to 49.6% (Issaquena) — a wider spread than between the best and worst U.S. states.
